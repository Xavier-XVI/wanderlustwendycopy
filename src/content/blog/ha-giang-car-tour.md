Ha Giang is the kind of place travel writers run out of adjectives for. The mountain passes, the rice terraces, the sheer verticality of the landscape — it's genuinely difficult to photograph in a way that captures the scale. You kind of have to be there.

We chose to do it by car rather than motorbike. I want to be upfront about that, because the motorbike option is what you'll see most often recommended online, and I understand the appeal. But it was rainy season, the roads were wet, and we saw two accident scenes in five days. The car felt like the right call.

We booked through **YESD** (Your Experience, Sustainable and Diverse), a social enterprise focused on responsible tourism in northern Vietnam. They were excellent.

## Why car, not motorbike

The Ha Giang Loop by motorbike is dramatic and beautiful and genuinely dangerous. The roads are narrow, the drops are steep, and the conditions change fast. If you're an experienced rider in good weather, it's probably fine. In rainy season, with inexperienced riders, it's genuinely risky.

The car option is slower in some ways — you can't stop quite as freely — but it comes with significant advantages: a knowledgeable local driver, a guide who could translate and contextualize everything, and the ability to sit in the back and watch the landscape without also managing a vehicle.

For two people with limited motorbike experience, it was the right choice.

## Day 1: Arrival in Thon Ha Village

We arrived in light rain that would persist for most of Day 1. Our first homestay was a traditional stilted cottage in Thon Ha village — wooden construction, simple but clean, surrounded by rice fields.

The landscape here has a strong resemblance to Yunnan Province in China, which makes sense: the Chinese border is 17km away. The cultural influences are layered and visible.

## Day 2: Markets, Heaven's Gate, and the Hemp Cooperative

The highlight of Day 2 was the Sunday market in **Phuong Do** — the kind of local market that exists for residents, not tourists, selling everything from livestock to mobile phone cases. We were the only foreigners there and I appreciated that our guide helped us understand what we were seeing rather than just narrating it.

In the afternoon we drove up to **Quan Ba Heaven's Gate** at 1500m elevation, with views of the valley and the distinctive twin hills known as Fairy Breast Mountain. Then to a women's hemp cooperative where we watched traditional textile production — hemp grown, processed, woven, and dyed by the women of the village.

We ended the day in **Nam Dam**, a government-designated tourism village with rice terrace views that were worth the designation.

## Day 3: The Hmong King Palace and Sunset Views

The weather improved and Day 3 was the most historically rich. The **Hmong King Palace** — built in 1907 by the powerful Vuong family — is a remarkable structure, part Chinese, part French colonial, financed substantially through the opium trade. The guide's explanation of the political history was detailed and didn't sanitize anything.

We also visited the ruins of a French military fortress before winding through a series of villages as the light got long and the views opened up for a sunset hike.

## Day 4: Ma Pi Leng Pass

The **Ma Pi Leng mountain pass** is the centerpiece of the Ha Giang Loop, and it deserves its reputation. The 6km hiking trail runs along the edge of a cliff above the Nho Que River, built by ethnic minority workers with hand tools and largely unchanged since.

It's not technically difficult — the trail is well-worn — but it demands attention. The drop on one side is significant. We passed village families using it as a regular commute, which put things in perspective.

The afternoon took us through **Meo Vac** and **Yen Minh** on increasingly remote roads. The landscape got more dramatic with every kilometer.

## Day 5: Waterfalls and Departure

The final day started with a hike to **Du Gia Waterfall**, which turned into an impromptu swim with local kids who had clearly done this before and were significantly more coordinated in the water than we were. A good moment.

We circled back to Heaven's Gate for better weather and better views than Day 2, then boarded an overnight bus to Sapa.

## The costs

The total for the five-day, four-night tour was **$732 for two people**, all-inclusive: guide, driver, accommodation in family-run homestays, most meals, and all transfers within the tour.

This works out to about $73 per person per day for a fully guided, immersive experience in one of Vietnam's most remote regions. For what it is, it's fair.

## Booking YESD

YESD is not the easiest company to find through a standard Google search — the best way to reach them is via Facebook, where they're responsive and can customize itineraries around your timeline, interests, and transportation preferences.

I'd recommend them without reservation. The guides spoke English well, the drivers were excellent on genuinely difficult roads, and the homestay hosts were welcoming in a way that felt genuine rather than performed. The focus on responsible tourism — keeping money in the communities you visit, avoiding extractive tourism models — was evident throughout.

Ha Giang rewards slow travel and local knowledge. YESD provided both.
