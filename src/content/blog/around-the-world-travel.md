One year ago, we landed in Vietnam and our around-the-world trip ended. I've been meaning to write this up properly ever since.

The numbers first, because that's what everyone wants to know: 397 days, six continents, **$42,590 total for two people** — an average of **$54 per person per day**.

Now the rest of it.

## How We Planned It

We didn't book everything in advance. We had a framework, not a fixed itinerary, and we planned detail roughly one month ahead at a time. Trying to plan more than that was, as I discovered early, overwhelming and often pointless — things change, things get cheaper, you meet people who tell you to go somewhere you hadn't considered.

The broad approach looked like this:

1. **Research and rough mapping** — travel blogs and guides to identify the shape of the route
2. **Accommodation research** — comparing Airbnb, hostels, and hotels via Booking.com for cost and neighborhood
3. **Flights** — Google Flights and Skyscanner exclusively. We set alerts and waited for prices to drop.
4. **Cost estimation** — drafting rough daily budgets for each region before committing
5. **Loyalty program review** — we used Award Wallet to track miles and points across all our programs, then identified where we could redeem for outsized value
6. **Itinerary adjustment** — after running numbers, shifting the route based on what we could afford and what we could cover with points
7. **Booking** — prioritizing refundable accommodation where possible
8. **Pre-departure mapping** — saving locations, neighborhoods, restaurants, and transit options to Google Maps before leaving each city
9. **Improvisation on arrival** — the actual day-to-day was largely unscheduled

The last point matters more than it sounds. We'd typically have a destination for the day and a rough sense of the area. From there: walking, following things that looked interesting, stopping when something was good. Most of the best experiences were unplanned.

France looks cheap because we were staying with family for most of it. The Trans-Siberian looks expensive because the train itself is a significant cost compressed into a short period.

### How we funded it

We kept roughly $80,000 in high-interest savings accounts before we left — enough to cover a year of travel without earning income, and enough that we weren't panicking every time we splurged on a nice dinner.

This freed us up to invest opportunistically during market downturns during the trip rather than liquidating everything in advance. It also eliminated the psychological pressure of counting every dollar, which I think is underrated as a travel quality-of-life factor.

### Travel rewards

Travel hacking is real and it made a meaningful difference. We estimate roughly **$20,000+ in value** from airline miles and hotel points across the trip — flights on American and Delta, stays via Hyatt, Marriott, and IHG rewards.

Without those points, we wouldn't have stayed at some of the properties we stayed at. With them, we had nights in places that genuinely felt like breaks from the road rather than just different beds.

### What we stopped paying for

One of the less-discussed financial realities of long-term travel: you eliminate a lot of fixed costs from your normal life. No rent. No gym membership. No regular salon appointments. No eating out of habit rather than appetite.

We adopted a strict one-in-one-out policy for physical possessions, which also helped keep the budget honest.

## The Itinerary

**June 2018:** Trans-Siberian Railway — Beijing to Ulaanbaatar to Irkutsk

**July:** Continuing the Trans-Siberian into Russia, then west through Ukraine, Poland, Germany, into France

**August:** Les Vosges region, Paris, then transatlantic to Washington D.C. and New York

**September:** Vermont, Montreal, Maine, Boston, New York, Washington D.C.

**October:** Las Vegas, Utah and Arizona national parks, San Francisco Bay Area

**November:** St. Louis, Kansas City, Houston

**December:** Buenos Aires, northern Argentina, Patagonia south to Ushuaia

**January 2019:** Uruguay, including a Workaway farm exchange that was one of the highlights of the trip

**February:** Chile — Santiago, Valparaíso, Atacama Desert

**March:** A Royal Caribbean cruise (we had points), then St. Louis, Detroit

**April–May:** Stationary in St. Charles and St. Louis — planning the final leg, decompressing

**June:** Paris, London, Munich, Zurich, Lyon — then Vietnam, where we settled

One thing shaped the entire year: we structured the route around friends and family. Almost every major stop had someone we knew at the other end. It made the travel more grounded and less lonely than it might have been otherwise.

## What long-term travel actually feels like

I want to be honest about this, because the fantasy version of full-time travel is very different from the lived version.

It's tiring. Not in a dramatic way, but in a cumulative way. The logistics of constant movement — packing, orienting to a new city, navigating transport, finding food, washing clothes, managing communications across time zones — takes up more energy than it sounds like on paper. It is absolutely not a never-ending vacation.

It's also genuinely transformative in ways that are hard to articulate without sounding like a travel Instagram caption. Being consistently outside your comfort zone, week after week, does something to how you process new situations. You get faster at reading environments. You get more comfortable with uncertainty. You develop a different relationship with possessions and with home.

Whether that's worth a year of your life depends on who you are. For us, it was.

## What I'd tell someone planning this

- Build more buffer into your budget than you think you need. Unexpected costs happen.
- Plan the shape of the route, not every day. Leave room for things to go wrong and for things to go better than expected.
- Anchor the route around people you want to see. The connections made the trip; the sights were secondary.
- Don't underestimate the mental cost of constant decision-making. Rest days are productive days.
- The Trans-Siberian Railway deserves a dedicated post. I'll write it eventually.

The trip ended. We moved to Saigon. Life kept going, different but still continuous. I'm glad we did it when we did.

If you're considering it — even a shorter version, a few months rather than a year — I'd encourage you to start planning. The logistics are solvable. The hardest part is deciding to actually go.
